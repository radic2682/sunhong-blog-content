:::note
**Key points in three lines**
- The M extension adds 4 multiplies (`mul` `mulh` `mulhsu` `mulhu`), 4 divides (`div` `divu` `rem` `remu`), and 5 W instructions on RV64. The lower half of a product is the same regardless of sign, so there is one `mul`; the upper half depends on interpretation, so there are three.
- Division by zero and signed overflow (−2³¹ ÷ −1) also produce defined values without exceptions. For divide-by-zero the quotient is all ones and the remainder is the dividend; for overflow the quotient is the dividend and the remainder is 0.
- Running 1,948 boundary pairs on QEMU, all matched a Python reference model. Without M, each multiply costs 86 extra instructions (a libgcc call), and each divide about 160 more.
:::

The RV32I we saw in earlier parts has neither multiplication nor division. You can build multiplication from adds, shifts, and compares, but it is slow. From part 7 on, we add extensions one at a time. The first is the M extension, which adds integer multiply and divide. It has few instructions, but the defined results RISC-V produces in cases that "normally raise an exception," such as division by zero and overflow, carry straight into the design. This part checks the meaning of the instructions and the boundary rules on QEMU, and leaves the multiplier and divider circuits to part 19 (implementing the M extension).

The environment is the same as part 3: a bare-metal program on QEMU 11.1.3's `virt` board, printing results over UART. The machine was a Mac M1 Max and the compiler `riscv64-elf-gcc` 16.2.

## 1. The M Extension at a Glance

All M extension instructions are R format, distinguished from base integer operations by opcode and funct7. They reuse opcode `OP` (0110011), which RV32I's `add` and `sub` use, and set funct7 to `0000001`. The three funct3 bits select the operation. The values below were taken directly from machine code produced by GNU as.

| funct3 | `OP` (0110011) | `OP-32` (0111011, RV64 only) |
|---|---|---|
| 000 | `mul` | `mulw` |
| 001 | `mulh` | |
| 010 | `mulhsu` | |
| 011 | `mulhu` | |
| 100 | `div` | `divw` |
| 101 | `divu` | `divuw` |
| 110 | `rem` | `remw` |
| 111 | `remu` | `remuw` |

The top bit of funct3 separates multiply (0) from divide (1). For the decoder, it becomes "if funct7 = 0000001 it is M, and inst[14] picks the multiplier or the divider." RV64's W instructions use the `OP-32` opcode like `addw` from part 5, and there are no W versions of the upper-half multiplies.

The specification explains why multiply and divide were left out of the base ISA: to save hardware in small implementations, or in applications where multiply and divide are rare or handed off to accelerators. So a core that implements only RV32I is a legitimate RISC-V core, and compilers for such cores turn multiplication into library calls. Section 6 measures that cost.

## 2. Multiplication: One Lower Half, Three Upper Halves

Multiplying two 32-bit numbers gives a 64-bit result. `mul` puts the lower 32 bits in `rd`. `mulh`, `mulhsu`, and `mulhu` put the upper 32 bits of the same product, differing in whether each operand is read as signed or unsigned.

| Instruction | rs1 read as | rs2 read as | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| `mul` | (doesn't matter) | (doesn't matter) | product [31:0] |
| `mulh` | signed | signed | product [63:32] |
| `mulhsu` | signed | unsigned | product [63:32] |
| `mulhu` | unsigned | unsigned | product [63:32] |

Why is there only one lower-half instruction? Let's follow the numbers. rs1 = `0xfffffff9`, rs2 = 7. Read as signed, −7 × 7 = −49, which in 64 bits is `0xffffffff_ffffffcf`. Read as unsigned, 4,294,967,289 × 7 = 30,064,771,023 = `0x00000006_ffffffcf`. The lower 32 bits are `0xffffffcf` in both, and only the upper 32 bits differ: `0xffffffff` and `0x00000006`.

![Multiplying rs1=0xfffffff9 by rs2=7, the lower half is 0xffffffcf under all three interpretations; only the upper half differs: 0xffffffff for mulh and mulhsu, 0x00000006 for mulhu|full](/img/01M6K8171K34F51SPYNF5R1VRH/39ee6be987b2.webp)

It is the same reason two's complement addition does not need to know the sign. The lower n bits of an n-bit product are determined only by the two numbers modulo 2ⁿ, and the signed and unsigned interpretations differ by a multiple of 2ⁿ. The difference in the upper half can be written as follows. The unsigned value $a_u$ and the signed value $a_s$ are related by $a_u = a_s + 2^{32} a_{31}$.

$$\text{mulhu}(a, b) = \text{mulh}(a, b) + a_{31} \cdot b + b_{31} \cdot a \pmod{2^{32}}$$

In words, the upper half of the unsigned product is the upper half of the signed product plus "the other operand, added once more for each operand that was negative." In the example above, $a_{31} = 1$ and $b_{31} = 0$, so adding b = 7 to `mulh`'s `0xffffffff` (−1) gives 6. This matches QEMU's `mulhu` result `0x00000006`. Multiplier hardware usually widens the operands by one bit (to 33 bits), chooses sign extension or zero extension, and handles all three instructions with the same signed multiplier. Part 19 covers this structure.

What is `mulhsu` for? As the specification explains, it is used when multiplying large signed multi-word numbers, to multiply the most significant word, which holds the sign bit, by the unsigned lower words. Here are a few boundary values (values from running on QEMU).

```text
rs1 × rs2     mul         mulh        mulhu
-1 × -1       0x00000001  0x00000000  0xfffffffe
min × min     0x00000000  0x40000000  0x40000000
-7 × 7        0xffffffcf  0xffffffff  0x00000006
```

min is `0x80000000` (−2³¹). (−2³¹)² = 2⁶², so `mulh` is `0x40000000`, and 2³¹ × 2³¹ read as unsigned is also 2⁶², so `mulhu` is the same. −1 × −1 is 1 as signed, so the upper half is 0, but read as unsigned it is (2³² − 1)², so the upper half is `0xfffffffe`.

## 3. Division: Truncate Toward Zero, Remainder Takes the Dividend's Sign

`div` and `divu` produce the quotient, and `rem` and `remu` the remainder. Signed division truncates the quotient toward zero (discarding the fractional part). As a result, the remainder has the same sign as the dividend (the number being divided). This is the same rule as C's `/` and `%`.

| Expression | Quotient (`div`) | Remainder (`rem`) | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| −7 ÷ 2 | −3 (`0xfffffffd`) | −1 (`0xffffffff`) | −3 × 2 − 1 = −7 |
| 7 ÷ −2 | −3 (`0xfffffffd`) | 1 | −3 × −2 + 1 = 7 |
| −7 ÷ 2 (unsigned) | `0x7ffffffc` | 1 | `divu`, `remu` |

The third row reads the same bits `0xfffffff9` as the unsigned 4,294,967,289. Same operands, yet the quotients are completely different: −3 and 2,147,483,644. So unlike the lower half of multiplication, division needs separate signed and unsigned versions.

## 4. Division by Zero and Overflow: Defined Values, No Exceptions

This is the most important rule in the M extension. Many ISAs trap on division by zero (x86's `#DE`). RISC-V does not. Instead, it defines the results.

| Case | Quotient `div[u]` | Remainder `rem[u]` |
|---|---|---|
| x ÷ 0 | All ones | x |
| −2³¹ ÷ −1 (`div`/`rem` only) | −2³¹ | 0 |

These are the values from running on RV32 QEMU.

```text
div  7 / 0          -> 0xffffffff
divu 7 / 0          -> 0xffffffff
rem  7 / 0          -> 0x00000007
remu 0xfffffff9 / 0 -> 0xfffffff9
div  0x80000000 / -1 -> 0x80000000
rem  0x80000000 / -1 -> 0x00000000
divu 0x80000000 / 0xffffffff -> 0
```

![The divider just selects: if b=0, quotient 0xffffffff and remainder a; if a=−2³¹ and b=−1, quotient a and remainder 0; otherwise the truncated-toward-zero result. There is no trap path|full](/img/01M6K8171K34F51SPYNF5R1VRH/e2baeb9e5362.webp)

These values were not picked arbitrarily. There are three reasons.

- **The identity holds.** For divide-by-zero, quotient × 0 + remainder = x; for overflow, −2³¹ × −1 + 0 = 2³¹ ≡ −2³¹ (wrapping around in 32 bits). Dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder always holds within 32 bits.
- **It is the natural output of an unsigned divider.** Restoring division does "if you can subtract, subtract and set the quotient bit to 1" at every step. If the divisor is 0, you can always subtract, so every quotient bit becomes 1 and the dividend is left as is in the remainder register. The specification says it defined signed division with the same value (all ones = −1) to make reusing the unsigned circuit easy.
- **There are no traps at all.** To condense the specification's explanation: adding a divide-by-zero trap would make it the only arithmetic trap in the standard ISA. If a language requires an exception, a single `beqz` after the divide does it, and since that branch is almost never taken, the cost is small.

GCC did not insert a zero check for C's `a / b` (the `divmod` output in section 7 is just two lines, `div` and `rem`). That is because division by zero in C is undefined behavior. Languages that need a zero check (e.g., Java's `ArithmeticException`) have the compiler insert the branch itself.

## 5. RV64: W Instructions and the 128-Bit Product

In RV64M, the eight instructions such as `mul` and `div` operate on 64 bits, and there are separate 32-bit versions: `mulw`, `divw`, `divuw`, `remw`, and `remuw`. The rule is the same as for the W instructions in part 5. **Compute using only the lower 32 bits, then sign-extend the 32-bit result to 64 bits.** Whatever is in the upper 32 bits is ignored.

I checked with `g = 0x12345678_7fffffff` (garbage on top, `0x7fffffff` below).

```text
mulw  g * 7     -> 0x000000007ffffff9
divw  g / 0     -> 0xffffffffffffffff
remw  g / 0     -> 0x000000007fffffff
remuw 0x80000000 / 0  -> 0xffffffff80000000
divuw 0x80000000 / -1 -> 0x0000000000000000
divw  0x80000000 / -1 -> 0xffffffff80000000
```

The fourth line is the trap. Even though `remuw` is an "unsigned" remainder, the result `0x80000000` was **sign-extended** to `0xffffffff80000000`. This is the part the specification nails down: "REMW and REMUW always sign-extend the 32-bit result to 64 bits, including on a divide by zero." The divide-by-zero quotient of `divuw` is also `0xffffffff` sign-extended, so all 64 bits become 1. The principle from part 5, that RV64 registers carry 32-bit values in sign-extended form, applies to unsigned operations too.

GCC compiled C `int` multiplication and division on RV64 to a single `mulw`, `divw`, or `remuw`. A 128-bit product of two 64-bit values (`unsigned __int128`) became two instructions, `mulhu` + `mul`, with the same structure as the 64-bit product on RV32. I checked the boundary rules for 64-bit `div` and `rem` the same way: for −2⁶³ ÷ −1, the quotient was `0x8000000000000000` and the remainder 0.

## 6. Hands-On: QEMU Results vs. a Reference Model, and the Cost Without M

**Correctness check.** I ran the 8 RV32M instructions on all 81 pairs of 9 boundary values (0, 1, −1, 2, −2, 7, −7, 2³¹−1, −2³¹), and on RV64 the 13 instructions on 100 pairs of 10 values. Each instruction was inserted with inline assembly, and inputs were held in `volatile` variables so the compiler could not precompute them.

```c
#define OP(op, a, b) ({ uint32_t r_; \
  __asm__ volatile(op " %0,%1,%2" \
    : "=r"(r_) : "r"(a), "r"(b)); r_; })
for (int i = 0; i < 9; i++)
  for (int j = 0; j < 9; j++) {
    volatile uint32_t a = V[i], b = V[j];
    show("mulh", a, b, OP("mulh", a, b));
    show("div", a, b, OP("div", a, b));
    /* … all 8 */
  }
```

I wrote the reference model directly with Python integers. Multiplication multiplies at infinite precision and then truncates; division takes `abs(a) // abs(b)`, applies the sign, and handles divide-by-zero and overflow separately up front.

```python
def div(a, b):
    a, b = sx(a, n), sx(b, n)
    if b == 0: return M          # all ones
    if a == -(1 << (n-1)) and b == -1:
        return a & M             # quotient = dividend
    q = abs(a) // abs(b)         # truncate toward zero
    return (-q if (a < 0) != (b < 0) else q) & M
```

All 648 RV32M cases (8 × 81 pairs) and 1,300 RV64M cases (13 × 100 pairs) matched the reference model, and the 13 encodings in the section 1 table also matched GNU as output.

**How much does it cost without M?** I compiled a loop doing multiplies and divides on 1,000 random pairs (fixed-seed LCG, a over the full 32 bits, b in 1~65,535) with three `-march` settings, launched QEMU with `-icount shift=0`, and counted with `minstret` (instructions executed). Optimization was `-O2`; the builds without M call libgcc's `__mulsi3`, `__divsi3`, and so on. All three builds produced the same computed result (checksum).

![Averaged over 1,000 iterations, rv32i uses 93 instructions per iteration for a*b, 165 for a/b, and 340 for quotient and remainder, while rv32im uses 7~9. Zmmul matches rv32im for multiplication only|xl](/img/01M6K8171K34F51SPYNF5R1VRH/651694f23496.webp)

| Operation (per iteration) | rv32i | rv32i_zmmul | rv32im |
|---|---|---|---|
| `a ^ b` (baseline) | 7.0 | 7.0 | 7.0 |
| `a * b` | 93.2 | 7.0 | 7.0 |
| 64-bit product | 207.3 | 9.0 | 9.0 |
| `a / b` (signed) | 165.2 | 165.2 | 7.0 |
| `a / b`, `a % b` | 339.6 | 339.6 | 9.0 |

Subtracting the baseline loop (xor), each multiply costs 86.2 extra instructions, each signed divide 158.2, and computing both quotient and remainder 332.6. With hardware multiply, a single `mul` takes the place of the xor, so the instruction count does not grow. These are instruction counts, not cycle counts. How many cycles `mul` and `div` take on a real core depends on the implementation, and division in particular commonly takes many cycles.

## 7. Instruction Pairing Order and Zmmul

**When you need both the high and low parts.** If you need the full 64-bit product (128-bit on RV64), the specification recommends this order.

```text
mulh[[s]u] rdh, rs1, rs2
mul        rdl, rs1, rs2
# same source order, and rdh must differ from rs1 and rs2
div[u]     rdq, rs1, rs2
rem[u]     rdr, rs1, rs2
# rdq must differ from rs1 and rs2
```

Written this way, the microarchitecture can merge the two instructions into a single multiply (or a single divide) (macro-op fusion). The reason for the conditions is simple. If the first instruction overwrites a source register, the second instruction's inputs change, and it is no longer the same product.

GCC 16.2's actual output got it half right.

```text
umul64:                 divmod:
  mv    a5,a1             div  a5,a0,a1
  mulhu a1,a0,a1          rem  a0,a0,a1
  mul   a0,a0,a5          sw   a5,0(a2)
  ret                     sw   a0,0(a3)
```

`divmod` is exactly the recommended form (`rdq`=a5 differs from the sources, and the source order is the same). In `umul64`, by contrast, `mulhu` wrote its result to `a1`, the same as rs2. So `mul` had to use the copy in `a5`, and the two instructions' sources became (a0, a1) and (a0, a5), which does not meet the fusion condition. It seems to be because the calling convention requires the 64-bit result to be returned in a1:a0 (an estimate). It may differ if the function is inlined and the register constraints are relaxed.

GCC also uses `mulhu` for division by a constant. `a / 10` (unsigned) became the magic constant `0xcccccccd` with `mulhu` and `srli 3` instead of `div`, because in many implementations an upper-half multiply is much faster than a divide. For `a / 8` (signed), to truncate negative values toward zero, it added 7 using `srai`, `andi`, and `add`, then did `srai 3`.

**Zmmul.** This extension takes only the multiply instructions from M (`mul` `mulh` `mulhsu` `mulhu`, plus `mulw` on RV64). The encodings are the same as M, and M includes Zmmul. The specification gives examples such as microcontrollers, where division is rare and divider area is not worth it, and FPGA soft cores, where a multiplier exists as a hard block but a divider would have to be built from logic. The `rv32i_zmmul` column in the section 6 table shows exactly this: multiplication matches rv32im, and division calls `__divsi3` and `__modsi3` like rv32i.

## 8. Design Checklist: The M Extension

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Decode | opcode `OP`/`OP-32`, funct7 = `0000001`. inst[14] selects multiply (0) or divide (1) |
| The three `mulh` variants | Sign- or zero-extend operands to 33 bits and use one multiplier. `mulhsu` signs rs1 only |
| Divide by zero | Quotient all ones, remainder = dividend. Same for `div` and `divu`. No exception |
| Overflow | `div`: −2^(XLEN−1) ÷ −1 → quotient = dividend, remainder 0. No exception |
| Remainder sign | If nonzero, the dividend's sign. The quotient truncates toward zero |
| W instructions | Use only the lower 32 bits and sign-extend the 32-bit result. Including `remuw` and `divuw` |
| W boundary values | Decide with L = 32: `divw` overflows when the lower 32 bits are `0x80000000` and `0xffffffff` |
| Trap path | M instructions raise no exceptions other than illegal instruction. A result selector is all you need |
| Multi-cycle | If division takes multiple cycles, stall instructions that depend on rd until the result is written |
| Fusion (optional) | Whether to fuse `mulh`+`mul` and `div`+`rem` is up to the implementation. Check the source and rd conditions |
| Zmmul only | Unimplemented divide encodings normally raise an illegal instruction exception |

The "trap path" row matters a lot for the design. Since no arithmetic exception comes out of anywhere in the integer execution units, you can split the multiplier and divider off as separate multi-cycle units without worrying about precise exceptions (part 27). The result simply arrives late; there is no case where "a trap happens midway and you have to roll back." The divide-by-zero check is also sometimes used for early termination: look at whether the divisor is 0 before starting the divide, and select the result right away. The specification defines only the result value; how many cycles it takes is up to the implementation.

## Summary

- The M extension has 4 multiplies, 4 divides, and 5 W instructions on RV64. The lower half of a product is sign-independent, so there is one `mul`; the upper half has three: `mulh`, `mulhsu`, and `mulhu`.
- Signed division truncates toward zero, and the remainder follows the dividend's sign. Divide-by-zero (quotient all ones, remainder the dividend) and overflow (quotient the dividend, remainder 0) are defined values without exceptions.
- The W instructions sign-extend their results, even the unsigned `remuw` and `divuw`. All 1,948 QEMU runs matched the reference model.
- Without M, each multiply costs 86 extra instructions and each divide about 160. The `mulh`+`mul` and `div`+`rem` orders are the forms recommended for fusion, and GCC produced that form only for division.
